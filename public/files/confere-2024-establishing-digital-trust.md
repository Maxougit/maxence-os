## 1. Abstract

In an era where the line between virtual and reality increasingly blurs, distinguishing authentic digital content from fabrications has never been more critical. In this paper, we introduce a novel authentication system designed to verify the provenance of digital content, distinguishing between AI-generated and human-originated content. We present and analyse a proof of concept demonstrating our system's preliminary efficacy, setting the stage for tackling expected challenges. Finally, we highlight the importance of discussing scalability, adaptability to emerging technologies, and accuracy, among other factors, to ensure the system's robustness and reliability in the ever-evolving digital landscape.

**Keywords:** Generative AI, Differentiation, Trust Authority, Certificate

## 2. Introduction

With the emergence of Artificial Intelligence (AI) technologies becoming publicly known towards the latter part of 2022, the digital landscape is on the brink of an unparalleled transformation. This shift is not solely technological: it represents a significant change in the conceptualization, production, and distribution of content across various modalities. The rapid advancement of AI technologies presents both opportunities and challenges, notably in preserving the equilibrium between innovation and integrity. A critical issue that has arisen is the growing difficulty in differentiating content generated by AI from that created by humans [12, 14]. This challenge extends beyond academic interest, affecting crucial sectors such as journalism, education, and knowledge dissemination at large.

Historical precedents, such as the manipulation of photographs during the Spanish Civil War or the recent surge in 'deepfake' videos, highlight the potential of technology to erode media trust. Currently, with AI at the forefront, tools like GPT-3.5, DALL-E, and their successors are blurring the distinctions between human and machine creativity, raising concerns about authenticity, reliability, and the preservation of human originality.

This paper aims to illuminate the necessity of distinguishing AI-generated multimodal content from human-generated content in the digital era. This distinction is crucial for preserving the integrity of information and sustaining the fundamental trust essential for digital interactions. Through an exploration of the development of generative AI technologies, the detailed aspects of multimodal content, and the consequences of their indistinguishability, this paper aims to address and elucidate the complexities associated with this contemporary issue.

## 3. State of the art

The challenge of distinguishing AI-generated content from human-generated content has emerged as a critical field of research since the public unveiling of generative AI technologies at the end of 2022. This section delves into significant contributions to this area, highlighting recent advancements and ongoing challenges through a critical analysis of key works.

Elkhatat et al. [4] and Chaka [2] have laid the groundwork for our current understanding, exploring the technological nuances underlying the challenge of differentiating AI-generated content from human creations. Their research emphasizes the variable efficacy of AI content detection tools, revealing a spectrum of success and limitations. While some tools demonstrate remarkable performance under specific conditions, others struggle against the advanced capabilities of recent AI models, highlighting the evolving nature of this challenge.

Rudolph et al. [9] have deepened the exploration of AI-generated content's impact in educational settings, underscoring the crucial need for robust detection mechanisms. Their work illuminates the risks AI poses to academic integrity, calling for close collaboration between educational institutions and technology developers to forge solutions that preserve honesty and authenticity in academic endeavors.

Conversely, Uzun et al. [13] investigate AI's potential to enhance the creative process while warning against its misuse. Their analysis uncovers the double-edged nature of AI-generated content, offering both opportunities for innovation and challenges in maintaining the authenticity and originality of human creation.

Lastly, Weber-Wulff et al. [15] or Andrei et al. [10] provide a comprehensive assessment of existing AI content detection tools. Their critical review not only evaluates the performance of these tools but also explores the methodologies behind them, suggesting how advancements in AI technology might necessitate new detection and verification approaches.

Together, these studies illuminate the dynamic interplay between technological innovation and the ethical, legal, and social implications of AI-generated content. As AI continues to permeate various aspects of society, the work of these researchers forms a crucial knowledge base for future research, laying the groundwork for continued exploration and development in this field, aiming to balance the benefits of AI with the imperatives of trust, integrity, and authenticity in the digital age.

## 4. Methodology

Our study introduces an innovative architecture designed to tackle the challenge of identifying AI-generated content. This architecture integrates technological and analytical methods across four key components: an Artificial Intelligence (AI) Model, the Applier, the Certifier, and a Social Network Interface. Below, we detail the functionality and role of each component within the system, emphasizing the technical mechanisms employed to ensure content integrity.

### 4.1 The proposed certification system

![Figure 1 — Schéma du système de certification proposé : les modèles d'IA génèrent du contenu, l'Applier y appose un marquage invisible (audio, image, texte), le contenu est publié sur les réseaux sociaux qui demandent au Certifier de délivrer un certificat « Is AI content » ou « Isn't AI content ».](/images/publications/confere-2024-fig1-systeme-certification.png)

*Figure 1. Schema of the proposed certification system*

The architecture uses a model that marks content generated by AI just after their generation (Applier) and the other can detect these specific markings to certify whether the content is generated by AI or not. Figure 1 presents the Applier and the Certifier as the new components used by the AI generator models and the social networks.

#### 4.1.1 Artificial Intelligence Models

In our architecture (Figure 1), AI Models refer to any advanced generative AI technologies capable of producing content, including, but not limited to, GPT-4, Mistral, LLAMA, and similar AGI systems. These models have the potential to generate highly realistic text [5, 7], images [8], videos, and other forms of content, posing a significant challenge for distinguishing between AI-generated and human-created outputs. Our methodology involves analyzing the capabilities and output characteristics of these models to develop more effective detection and verification strategies.

#### 4.1.2 Applier

The Applier is a sophisticated machine designed as the first layer of defense against AI-generated content. It employs a variety of techniques to mark content with subtle indicators of its origin, such as watermarks [11], subpixel variations, or specialized metadata.

This preprocessing step flags potential AI-generated content early in the pipeline, facilitating further review and verification. The design of the Applier focuses on minimally invasive methods to ensure that the integrity and quality of the content are preserved while still providing a reliable means of initial identification.

#### 4.1.3 Certifier

The Certifier is a more advanced layer of our architecture, capable of detailed analysis and final verification of content flagged by the Applier or not. It may utilize a combination of machine learning models and heuristic approaches to recognize the watermarks, sub-pixel patterns, and metadata inserted by the Applier.

The Certifier can analyze content characteristics such as text structure, image composition, and video metadata to assess authenticity. Accessible via an API, the Certifier offers services for external entities to verify whether content is AI-generated. This component ensures a high level of accuracy in distinguishing between AI-generated and human-generated content through comprehensive analysis.

#### 4.1.4 Social Network Interface

The Social Network Interface is designed as a universally applicable framework to facilitate the integration of our content verification system with a broad spectrum of social media platforms, including but not limited to Twitter, Instagram, and Facebook. This component is crucial for the dissemination of verified information across the digital landscape, enabling a seamless application of content authenticity labels or warnings on a global scale.

In recognizing the profound social implications of AI-generated content, our approach prioritizes transparency and accessibility for all users across these platforms. The interface ensures that users can easily discern AI-generated content, supporting informed engagement and interaction. By offering a standardized API, we provide social networks with the capability to automatically label content verified by our system, indicating whether it is AI-generated or human-created.

This initiative is not merely a technical solution but a step towards fostering a digital environment where trust is paramount, and the authenticity of content is clear and discernible. It empowers users with knowledge about the origin of the content they consume, promoting critical engagement and helping to mitigate the potential social impacts of misleading or inauthentic content.

### 4.2 Proof of Concept

To validate the practical feasibility and effectiveness of our proposed content certification system, we developed a Proof of Concept (POC), as detailed in our GitHub repository [6]. The POC is designed to exemplify the core functionalities of our architecture, demonstrating the integration and interaction between the Artificial Intelligence Models, the Applier, the Certifier, and the Social Network Interface.

To realise the POC we are using a Python environment, recognized for its simplicity and effectiveness in deploying basic AI models, which are widely accessible as open-source and runnable via Python. This decision facilitates the demonstration of our conceptual framework in a straightforward manner. The POC implements a command-line interface that illustrates the various stages of the system in action.

It commences with a user requesting content generation from an AI model, operationalized through simple command-line inputs. Users have the flexibility to prompt the generation of either textual or visual content.

For textual content generation, we leverage the "mistral-7b" model by Mistral editors, executed via the Ollama Python library to produce high-quality text on a local machine. Conversely, for visual content, the "stable-diffusion-v1-5" model is employed, necessitating the Torch Python library for its execution, a choice made for its proficiency in handling deep learning applications.

Subsequent to content creation, it is imperative to embed markings within the content to facilitate later verification. The embedding methods vary by content type: for text, the "mistral-7b" model is preconfigured, via the system prompt, to integrate indiscernible AI patterns in content outputs; for images, post-generation, we apply the "invisible-watermark" Python library to insert a non-visible watermark (Figures 2 and 3).

![Figure 2 — Photographie d'un astronaute sur la Lune, générée par IA, avant application du filigrane invisible.](/images/publications/confere-2024-fig2-image-sans-filigrane.jpg)

*Figure 2. Picture without watermark*

![Figure 3 — La même photographie après insertion du filigrane invisible : aucune différence perceptible à l'œil nu.](/images/publications/confere-2024-fig3-image-avec-filigrane.jpg)

*Figure 3. Picture with watermark*

Using a script with the original image and the watermarked one, we can definitely highlight the difference between the two pictures and have a better look on how the system works (Figure 4). It also shows the potential counters that can be applied to obscure the check process.

![Figure 4 — Différence entre les deux images révélée par soustraction : le motif du filigrane apparaît en rouge sur toute la surface de l'image.](/images/publications/confere-2024-fig4-difference-couche-invisible.jpg)

*Figure 4. Difference in invisible picture layer*

Upon generation and marking, the user is free to utilize the content as desired. The POC simulates a scenario where the user posts this content on a social network. Before the content is published, the social network forwards it to a Verifier for certification.

The Verifier's certification process differs based on content type. For text, the Verifier uses a modified "mistral-7b" model to analyze the origin of the text, discerning human from AI-generated content through the detection of specific rules and patterns. For images, the "invisible-watermark" library is again utilized to detect AI-generated watermarks.

In culmination, the content is certified as human-generated or AI-generated. The results are communicated back to the social network, effectively completing the certification cycle outlined in the POC.

This transmission of information marks the successful conclusion of the certification process as delineated in the POC. Subsequently, the social network can utilize the certificate to annotate the user's post with an indication of its origin, thereby informing the wider user community and maintaining transparency within the digital ecosystem.

### 4.3 Evaluation and Implementation

The cornerstone of our research lies in the conceptualization and development of an architecture designed to distinguish between AI-generated and human-generated content. The evaluation of this architecture encompasses both its theoretical impact on digital content integrity and the practical feasibility of its implementation across various content modalities, with a special emphasis on image analysis.

#### 4.3.1 Theoretical Impact

Our initial phase of evaluation focuses on assessing the theoretical impact of our proposed architecture on enhancing the transparency and trustworthiness of digital content. This involves a comprehensive analysis of the potential benefits, challenges, and societal implications of implementing such a system. Through literature reviews, expert interviews, and scenario analyses, we aim to understand the broader context within which our architecture operates and its potential to mitigate the risks associated with AI-generated content.

#### 4.3.2 Feasibility Study

The feasibility study delves into the technical and operational aspects of realizing our architecture, particularly the processing of AI-generated images. Given the significant computational demands associated with image analysis, we explore a range of solutions to address these challenges. These solutions include leveraging cloud computing resources, optimizing algorithms for efficiency, and investigating state-of-the-art techniques in image processing and AI detection.

While the practical testing of these solutions is beyond the scope of our current research, we provide a detailed exploration of their theoretical effectiveness and potential integration into our architecture. This includes a critical examination of the trade-offs between computational efficiency and detection accuracy, as well as the scalability of the proposed solutions in real-world applications.

## 5. Results

The advent and proliferation of AI-generated content have far-reaching implications across misinformation dissemination, copyright issues, trust in digital media, and computational and environmental costs. This subsection synthesizes these impacts within the context of our findings, drawing upon recent research to underline the severity of these issues.

### 5.1 Experimental Setup

The experiment was carried out on a Python environment and the machine was equipped with an Apple Silicon M3. The CPU has a GPU with Metal Performance Shaders on SOC. In addition, the machine also had 16 GB unified memory. Some libraries were used such as scikit-learn, joblib, ollama, diffusers, torch, transformers, accelerate, invisible-watermark, opencv-python.

#### 5.1.1 Misinformation and Disinformation

The capacity of AI to generate convincing yet entirely fabricated content presents unprecedented challenges in misinformation and disinformation. Caceres et al. [1] have documented the significant impact of misinformation on public health during the COVID-19 pandemic, illustrating how false narratives can hinder pandemic response efforts and vaccine uptake. On a more specific subject, Todd et al. [12] explained the deepfake technology, a notable product of AI advancements, has further compounded these challenges by enabling the creation of persuasive yet false narratives. Such technologies not only have the potential to influence public opinion and political landscapes significantly but also pose a direct threat to public health and safety.

#### 5.1.2 Trust in Digital Media

As distinguishing between AI and human-generated content becomes increasingly challenging, public trust in digital media is at risk. This erosion of trust complicates the dissemination of reliable information, from news to public health advisories, potentially fostering an "information trust crisis." The implications of such a crisis are far-reaching, affecting not only the way information is consumed but also how it is valued in society.

### 5.2 Watermark

As demonstrated in Figure 2 and Figure 3, the implementation of our invisible watermarking technique for AI-generated images showcases the system's subtlety and effectiveness. Despite the additional security layer, there is no discernible difference between the image without a watermark and the image with a watermark. This ensures that the aesthetic and informational integrity of the visual content remains uncompromised while still providing a robust mechanism for content verification and authentication.

### 5.3 Computational Efficiency and Challenges

Our Proof of Concept (POC) showcases the system's operational efficiency through a series of tests focused on the encoding and decoding of watermarks, an important process in our content certification system. In the POC study, the average execution times for watermark processes were recorded as **0.0376 seconds for encoding** and **0.0233 seconds for decoding**. The average execution times for these processes are highlighting the system's capability for rapid content processing.

### 5.5 User Feedback and Social Implications

A common theme among the feedback was optimism about the system's potential to mitigate the spread of misinformation. Participants speculated that the widespread adoption of certification could create a more discerning audience and deter the spread of false information, particularly on social media platforms. Some respondents expressed hope that such a system could have prevented the rampant spread of misinformation witnessed during events like the COVID-19 pandemic.

### 5.6 Limitations and Future Directions

Our Proof of Concept (POC) for AI content detection, as illustrated in Figure 1, represents an initial step towards distinguishing AI-generated content from human-generated content. While it lays a foundational framework for identifying such content, several limitations have been identified through its development and preliminary testing.

#### 5.6.1 Scalability

One primary limitation observed is the POC's scalability. As the volume of content to be analyzed grows, especially in high-throughput environments like social media platforms, the need for more robust and scalable infrastructure becomes apparent. Future iterations will explore cloud-based solutions and distributed computing to enhance the system's ability to process large datasets efficiently.

#### 5.6.2 Adaptability to New AI Technologies

The rapid evolution of AI and generative technologies presents a moving target for detection systems. Our POC's current model may not fully capture the nuances of the latest or future AI-generated content. Ongoing research and model retraining are essential to keep pace with advancements in AI technologies, ensuring our system remains effective.

#### 5.6.3 Accuracy and False Positives

While our POC demonstrates promising accuracy levels, the occurrence of false positives — where human-generated content is mistakenly identified as AI-generated — remains a concern. Improving the model's precision will be a focus of future work, potentially through the integration of more sophisticated machine learning algorithms or deeper contextual analysis.

#### 5.6.4 User Privacy and Data Security

Ensuring user privacy and data security is paramount, especially as the system processes potentially sensitive content. Future development will prioritize the implementation of enhanced data protection measures and adherence to privacy regulations, aiming to build trust with users and stakeholders.

#### 5.6.5 Broadening Content Types

The current POC primarily addresses textual content and pictures, with limited capacity for huge input and other multimedia formats such as sound or video. Expanding the system's capabilities to include these content types is crucial, given the increasing prevalence of AI-generated voices and videos. This expansion will involve incorporating advanced computer vision and audio analysis techniques [3].

#### 5.6.6 Stakeholder Engagement and Standardization

For widespread adoption and impact, engaging with a broad spectrum of stakeholders — from tech companies to regulatory bodies — is essential. Future directions include efforts to standardize content certification processes and collaborate with industry partners to establish a universally recognized trust framework.

In conclusion, while our POC for AI content detection sets the groundwork for distinguishing between AI-generated and human-generated content, it is but the first step in an ongoing journey. Addressing its current limitations and exploring future directions will require a concerted effort from researchers, industry practitioners, and policymakers alike. By doing so, we can aspire to create a digital ecosystem where authenticity and trust prevail.

### 5.7 Points of Failure

Our system, while innovative, faces potential points of failure that could impact its effectiveness:

- **Model efficiency.** The rapid advancement of generative AI could outpace our model's detection capabilities, posing a risk to the system's long-term effectiveness.
- **Trust and integrity.** Trust in the certification process is crucial. Any errors or inconsistencies could undermine confidence in the system, highlighting the importance of accuracy and reliability.
- **System manipulation.** The potential for malicious actors to manipulate or bypass the content marking system poses a significant security challenge that must be addressed to maintain the system's integrity.
- **Computational demands.** The accuracy of content detection is tied to computational resources. Balancing computational efficiency with detection accuracy without compromising the system's scalability is a critical concern.

Addressing these points of failure is essential for the system's success and will be a focus of our ongoing development efforts.

## 6. Analysis

### 6.1 Interpretation of Results

The results from our Proof of Concept (POC) and the computational efficiency assessments highlight the potential of our proposed certification system to significantly enhance the integrity and trustworthiness of digital content. The successful implementation of the Applier and Certifier models demonstrates a viable pathway to distinguishing AI-generated content from human-generated content.

However, the challenges identified, such as scalability and adaptability to new AI technologies, underscore the need for continuous refinement of our approach.

### 6.2 Comparison with Existing Methods

Compared to existing content verification methods, our system introduces a novel integration of AI models for the explicit purpose of content certification. While current tools focus predominantly on detecting deepfakes or synthetic media, our approach aims to certify content at the point of creation, offering a proactive solution to the challenge of digital trust. This strategic shift from detection to certification aligns with the evolving landscape of digital content creation and consumption, setting a new standard for trust and authenticity online.

### 6.3 Analysis of Computational Efficiency and Strategic Implementations

The development and implementation of our system confront several computational and operational challenges, crucial among them being the demands for high-resolution image and video analysis. To address these, we are exploring the integration of more efficient machine learning algorithms and investigating the potential role of quantum computing to enhance processing capabilities without sacrificing accuracy. A move towards a distributed computing framework appears promising as a scalable solution to accommodate the burgeoning volume of content necessitating certification.

Operational costs, driven predominantly by computational resource utilization and data storage, are a paramount concern. Our approach aims to optimize algorithm efficiency and capitalize on cloud-based solutions to provide scalable compute power. A tiered service model is proposed to ensure that the system is accessible to entities of varying sizes, promoting widespread adoption of content certification.

Technical integration and logistical coordination present significant challenges, particularly when embedding our system within the existing infrastructures of third-party companies. The development of flexible APIs and robust back-end systems, coupled with comprehensive SDKs and documentation, is critical to facilitate seamless adoption across different platforms and companies.

Looking forward, the system is designed with a keen eye on scalability and adaptability to future advancements in AI technology. This foresight is critical to maintain the system's relevance and effectiveness in a landscape where AI capabilities are rapidly evolving. Our ongoing Research and Development efforts are dedicated to ensuring the system's capability to handle increasing content volumes and adapt to emerging forms of AI-generated content, balancing the quest for accuracy with the practicalities of computational load and system viability.

### 6.4 Broader Implications

The broader implications of our research extend beyond the technical realm, touching on ethical, societal, and regulatory considerations. Establishing a universally accepted framework for content certification could transform the digital ecosystem, promoting a culture of transparency and accountability. However, achieving this goal requires concerted efforts from technology developers, platform operators, policymakers, and users to navigate the complex ethical and societal dynamics involved.

### 6.5 Limitations and Future Research

While our POC lays the groundwork for a new era of digital content certification, it is not without limitations. The scalability issues and the need for broader content type coverage highlight areas for future development. Additionally, engaging with a wider range of stakeholders to standardize the certification process and address privacy concerns will be critical. Ongoing research will also need to monitor the rapid advancements in AI technology to ensure the system's continued effectiveness.

## 7. Conclusion

In this article, we have proposed an innovative system aimed at certifying the verification of content posted online, with a particular emphasis on distinguishing between content generated by artificial intelligence (AI) and that created by humans. Our approach relies on the use of two distinct AI models developed by a trusted third party, allowing for the placement of marks on content and their reliable recognition.

One of these models is designed to integrate invisible or visible markings, such as steganography for sound and watermarks for images, into AI-generated content. When a user publishes content online, digital platforms are prompted to seek certification from a trusted authority responsible for verifying the authenticity of the content and assigning a label indicating whether it was generated by AI or not.

Our proposal poses significant challenges, including the mandatory adoption of the system by online platforms and companies marketing AI technologies. Additionally, it is crucial to consider the potential risks associated with errors in judgment by the certification authority and attempts at manipulation aimed at erasing the markings.

In conclusion, our content certification system represents a crucial step towards preserving the integrity of information and maintaining trust in the digital age. By ensuring transparency and reliability in online content, we contribute to building a digital environment where trust and authenticity prevail, allowing users to navigate safely in the ever-evolving digital landscape.
